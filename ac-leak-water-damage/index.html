How an air conditioner ends up leaking

Cooling air removes moisture, and that moisture has to go somewhere. It collects on the evaporator coil, drips into a pan and leaves through a condensate drain line. When any part of that path fails, the water takes the next easiest route into your home.

In Addison, cooling systems can run for long stretches through the summer. That gives a partly clogged line plenty of time to back up. If your air handler sits in an attic or a closet, a small overflow can show up first as a ceiling stain or a damp closet wall.

Clogged condensate line

Algae, slime and dust build up in the line. Once it blocks, water fills the primary pan and then the secondary pan, if your system has one.

Frozen coil that thaws

Low airflow or a dirty filter can ice the coil. When it thaws, a burst of water can overwhelm the pan. The ice melts all at once, so the leak looks sudden.

First-hour safety steps

Work from the thermostat outward, and stay out of anything that looks unsafe.

  • Turn the system to off at the thermostat, then switch off the unit's breaker or the disconnect near it.
  • Do not touch wiring or the unit if you are standing in water, or if water is dripping near the electrical panel.
  • Do not climb into a wet attic. Wet insulation and decking can give way, and attic heat adds its own risk.
  • Call 911 for sparking, burning smells or smoke. Call the gas utility if a gas furnace shares the closet and you smell gas.
  • Put a bucket or towels under active drips and take dated photos of the source and the stains.

Clean water that ages fast

Condensate starts as clean water, but it picks up dust and organic film from the coil and pan. That is why we handle it as clean water that ages fast. Left wet, insulation and board can start to smell within a short time, and the Texas Department of Insurance notes that mold can start growing within a day after a leak.

Our process for an AC leak

We follow industry practice under ANSI/IICRC S500, and we begin with readings.

  1. Map the moisture. A thermal camera and moisture meters show how far the water went through ceiling board, closet walls and flooring.
  2. Remove standing water. We extract pooled water from pans, closet floors and carpet with portable extractors.
  3. Take out what is soaked. Wet insulation and ceiling board that will not dry come out. We use flood cut tools only where the wall needs it.
  4. Dry the cavity. Air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and injectidry cavity drying work through the structure until readings reach a dry reference.
  5. Clean and record. HEPA air scrubbers help with dust during removal, and we document readings and photos for your insurer.

For the full rebuild after drying, see water damage repair. Longer drying jobs are explained under structural drying.

Insurance and AC leaks

Policies differ, so ask your agent or adjuster. A sudden overflow or burst is often viewed differently from damage that built up over a long time. Maintenance problems can complicate a claim.

Prevention and mistakes to avoid

Most AC leaks are preventable with small habits.

  • Ask your HVAC technician about a float switch that shuts the system off when the pan fills.
  • Have the condensate line flushed on a schedule, and check the pan for standing water.
  • Replace filters so the coil does not freeze.
  • Do not ignore a stain, even a small one, or keep running a system that is dripping.

If a musty smell lingers after drying, see mold remediation. We serve homes in Richardson and Coppell as well. The HVAC technician repairs the unit. We do not.
