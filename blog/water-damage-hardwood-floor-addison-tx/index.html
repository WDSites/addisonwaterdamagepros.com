Water Damage on a Hardwood Floor in Addison, TX: Cupping, Crowning, Buckling and What Comes Next

Wood takes on moisture slowly and gives it up slowly. What your boards are doing today tells you where the water came from and what to do first.

By the Addison Water Damage Pros team · Updated 2026

☎ (972) 945-2885

Water damage on a hardwood floor in Addison, TX shows up as cupped, crowned or buckled boards, dark stains, or gaps and soft spots. Wood swells when it absorbs moisture, and the shape of the swelling points to the source.

Some floors can be dried and saved, some need partial replacement, and some are a total loss. Speed matters, because boards that stay wet for days are harder to recover. This guide covers the three distortions, their usual causes, drying, and when to replace.

Reading the floor: sign, meaning and usual path

Look at the boards from a low angle with a light across the surface. The profile tells you a lot before any meter comes out.

SignWhat it usually meansTypical path
Cupping: board edges higher than the centerMore moisture on the underside than the top, often from a slow leak or damp slabFix the source, dry the floor, wait, then sand if the boards flatten
Crowning: center higher than the edgesMoisture from above, or a cupped floor that was sanded before it driedDry fully, then sand and refinish once readings are stable
Buckling: boards lifting off the subfloorHeavy, prolonged saturation, often from a large leak or floodEmergency drying, then expect partial or full replacement
Dark gray or black stainingWater sitting on the surface or in the grain for a long timeSanding may remove light stains, deep stains usually mean replacing boards
Gaps after dryingBoards that swelled, then shrank as they driedOften acceptable, repair only if gaps are wide or trip hazards

These are patterns, not diagnoses. A moisture meter and an inspection of the subfloor confirm them.

What usually causes each pattern

Wood floors answer the question of where the water came from if you know what to look for.

Washer supply valve or hose

A weeping valve behind the machine soaks the floor nearby. It often starts as slow cupping at the laundry room edge and spreads along the boards.

HVAC condensate line

A clogged line drips onto the floor or runs under the closet. This is a gradual leak, so cupping may appear weeks before anyone sees water.

Dishwasher or refrigerator line

The kitchen floor beside the dishwasher swells first. Damage may hide under cabinet toe kicks.

Slab moisture

Moisture moving up through a concrete slab cups boards across the whole room instead of in one spot. This is a building moisture problem, not a one-time leak.

Can the boards be saved?

Sometimes. It depends on how long the wood stayed wet, whether the water was clean, how the floor is installed, and how quickly drying began. No one can promise an outcome from a photograph.

Floors dried within a few days after a short, clean-water event have a fair chance of flattening. Cupping often relaxes over a period of weeks as boards equalize, and rushing a sand can lock in the distortion. Floors that sat wet for a week or more, were nailed over a damp subfloor, or took in contaminated water are harder cases.

Solid versus engineered wood

Solid hardwood can often be sanded more than once, so mild cupping that flattens is usually fixable. Engineered boards have a thin top layer over plywood or composite, and once the layers delaminate they cannot be repaired. Glued-down floors over concrete behave differently from nailed floors over a wood subfloor.

How a wood floor is dried

Wood drying is slow and controlled. Heating the room hard and blasting fans can dry the top of the board faster than the bottom, which makes cupping worse. The goal is steady moisture removal until the boards match a dry reference reading from an unaffected room.

Crews typically combine water mitigation tools: floor drying mats or panels that pull air through the wood, LGR dehumidifiers to lower humidity, and air movers set at a measured distance. Some systems apply gentle suction over a sealed section of floor. Moisture readings are logged each day, and equipment stays until the numbers settle.

Why the subfloor matters

A floor can read dry on top while moisture stays trapped in the subfloor or slab below. Drying that stops too soon can leave a floor that cups again after refinishing. Subfloor readings should be part of the check.

What you can do in the first day

These steps buy the floor time while you arrange help.

  • Stop the water at the source and turn off the supply to the leaking appliance.
  • Remove standing water with towels, a mop or a wet vacuum.
  • Lift rugs, mats and furniture legs off the wood, and put foil or plastic under any legs you cannot move.
  • Run the air conditioning to keep air moving, and avoid cranking up heat.
  • Photograph the damage and the source before repairs.
  • Do not sand, wax, or seal the floor until it has been measured dry.

When to replace instead of refinish

Replace boards that are soft, black through the grain, split or delaminated. Replace sections where buckling has pulled boards from the subfloor and they will not lie flat. Replace anything that sat in sewage or other contaminated water.

Refinishing comes last, after readings are stable. Sanding too soon removes wood from boards that may flatten on their own, and finish applied over damp wood can trap moisture. Replacement boards also need to acclimate and then be blended into the old floor, which usually means refinishing the whole room.
