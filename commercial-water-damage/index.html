How water moves through a commercial space

Water in a commercial property rarely stays where it started. It runs along floors, drops through ceiling tile and follows pipe chases and electrical openings. A leak on one floor can soak a suite below it and the corridor beside it.

Commercial finishes behave differently from a house. Suspended ceiling tile, metal studs with drywall, carpet tile over concrete and insulation in wall cavities each react to water in their own way. Some can be dried. Others have to come out.

Common sources

Supply lines, sprinkler or fire line discharge, roof drains, HVAC condensate, restroom overflows and storm water can all be the start. A plumber, roofer or building engineer repairs the source. We handle the water, the materials and the drying.

Why time matters more in a shared building

Wet materials in one suite can affect neighbors. Humidity moves through shared ceilings and walls. The sooner water is out and drying begins, the smaller the area that needs work.

First-hour safety steps

Commercial spaces have more electrical and mechanical systems than most homes, so slow down.

  • Keep everyone out of areas with standing water near panels, outlets or equipment.
  • Locate the water main with your property manager or building engineer and shut it off if you can do so safely.
  • Do not touch wet electrical gear. Call 911 for sparks, smoke or any shock risk.
  • If you smell gas, leave and call the gas utility and 911.
  • Do not use the elevator if water is near the shaft or machine room.
  • If the water came from a sewer or restroom overflow, treat it as contaminated and keep people away.

Photograph the source, the water and the damage before anything is moved. Note times and who you spoke to.

Our commercial restoration process

Every building is different, so the first step is always a walk-through.

  1. Scope the loss. We walk the space with your property manager, check where the water traveled and agree on a plan and the order of work.
  2. Extract standing water. Truck-mount and portable extractors pull water from carpet, hard floors and low areas. For more on the extraction stage, see emergency water removal.
  3. Map moisture. Thermal cameras and moisture meters show wet areas on walls, ceilings and slabs. We record readings room by room.
  4. Remove wet finishes. Ceiling tile, drywall, carpet tile and insulation that cannot dry are cut out and removed. Flood cut tools keep the cut lines clean.
  5. Dry and monitor. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run until readings meet a dry reference. The method is covered on our structural drying page.
  6. Clean and document. Surfaces are cleaned, HEPA air scrubbers filter the air if materials were opened, and we assemble photos and readings in a plain report.

Industry practice under ANSI/IICRC S500 guides how drying is documented, and that is how we keep records.

Working in phases

A building cannot always shut down. Where the layout allows, we can work in phases, sealing off the wet area with containment so the rest of the space stays usable. Whether that works depends on the size of the loss, the water category and how the space is laid out.

We will tell you plainly what is practical. A clean-water leak in one room is easier to separate than contaminated water that spread across a floor.

Insurance and commercial losses

Commercial policies are written in many ways, and coverage for water damage, sewer backup, flood and business interruption can differ. Policies differ, so ask your agent or adjuster which parts apply.

Sudden losses and gradual ones are often treated differently. Keep photos, moisture logs and invoices together. We provide readings and notes in a format a property manager or insurer can read.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting for the insurer before drying begins.
  • Leaving wet ceiling tile and insulation in place.
  • Running heat or air without checking how it affects humidity.
  • Throwing away damaged items before they are photographed.
  • Skipping a conversation with the property manager about shutoffs and access.
