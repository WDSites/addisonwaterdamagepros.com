While you wait, keep the heat on, keep people away from any ceiling that is bulging or dripping, and leave lights and outlets in the wet area switched off until the power has been checked.

Why a freeze becomes a flood after the thaw

Water expands as it turns to ice. That pressure can split a pipe or push a fitting apart, but the crack often stays sealed by the ice itself. The flooding starts when the temperature climbs and the plug melts. A quiet house at midnight can have water running inside a wall by noon.

Where the split happens depends on how your home is built. If a supply line runs through an exterior wall, an attic, an unheated garage or a crawl space, it has less warmth around it than a line in an interior wall. Outdoor hose bibs and irrigation lines can crack too, then leak back toward the house once the pipe thaws.

Rooms that were left unheated

A closed-off bedroom or a second home left empty during a cold week can lose enough heat for pipes to freeze. Water often soaks the insulation beside the pipe, and wet insulation holds moisture against framing for a long time.

First-hour safety steps

Water and wiring share the same walls in a freeze loss, so safety comes before cleanup.

  • Close the main water valve at your house if you can reach it. If the inside valve is stuck, call the Town rather than forcing the meter.
  • If water is near outlets, light fixtures or the panel, turn off power only if you can reach the breaker without stepping through water. Otherwise stay out and call an electrician or 911.
  • Stay out from under a ceiling that is bowed, sagging or dripping heavily. Trapped water can drop all at once.
  • Never use a torch, open flame or heat gun to thaw a pipe.
  • If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 from outside.

How we dry a freeze-related loss

Our work starts once the water is stopped, and it follows industry practice under ANSI/IICRC S500.

  1. Scope with a thermal camera and meters. We scan walls, ceilings and floors, then confirm with moisture meters. Melt water often travels farther than the stain suggests.
  2. Extract standing water. Truck-mount and portable extractors pull water from carpet and hard floors. Our emergency water removal page covers that stage in more detail.
  3. Open what cannot dry closed. Wet insulation comes out. Drywall that is soaked or sagging gets a flood cut above the wet line, and we leave the cavity open for airflow.
  4. Dry the cavity and the room. Air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and injectidry cavity drying ports move moisture out of framing. The structural drying page explains how that equipment is set up and read.
  5. Keep the heat on, then document. Warm air holds more moisture, so keep a normal thermostat setting while equipment runs. We return with meters until framing matches a dry reference reading, then give you photos and readings for your adjuster.

How insurance usually views a freeze loss

Many policies treat a sudden burst differently from slow, ongoing seepage. A pipe that split in the freeze and flooded a wall tends to fit the sudden category. A drip nobody noticed for weeks may not. Policies differ, so ask your agent or adjuster how yours reads.

Document before cleanup. Take wide photos of each room, close photos of the split pipe and the wet materials, and note the date and the cold snap. Keep the plumber's invoice describing the failure. We add moisture readings and a drying log.

Mistakes to avoid and what to do before the next freeze

  • Turning the heat down to save money while the house is wet.
  • Waiting to see whether a stain grows. The Texas Department of Insurance notes that mold can start growing within a day after a leak.
  • Drying only the surface with a household fan.
  • Assuming a thawed pipe is a fixed pipe. Have a plumber check the line.
  • Skipping prevention: disconnect hoses, turn sprinklers off, open cabinet doors on exterior walls and let a faucet drip when a hard freeze is forecast.

If visible growth does appear, see our mold remediation page for the next step. We work the same way for homes in Carrollton and Plano, which share the same cold fronts.
