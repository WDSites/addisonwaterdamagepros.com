How storm water gets into a North Texas home

A storm rarely floods a house from the front door. More often it finds the small gaps. Wind pushes rain sideways, and water that would normally run off a window frame or garage door seal gets forced into the wall instead.

Door thresholds, garage door bottoms and window sills are common entry points. Clogged gutters overflow at the fascia, and if the grading slopes toward an exterior wall, runoff follows it.

Hail adds a second path. It can crack shingles, dent flashing and split siding, and the opening may not leak until the next rain. If you see ceiling stains after a hailstorm, the roof leak page covers the path from the roof down.

First-hour storm safety steps

Power and water are a bad mix, and storms often damage both. Go through these before you touch anything.

  • Call 911 for a downed power line, a sparking outlet or anyone hurt, and stay back from the line.
  • Do not walk through standing water if outlets, cords, a panel or appliances are at or below the waterline.
  • If it is safe and dry where you stand, switch off the breakers for the wet area. Otherwise leave it for an electrician.
  • A gas smell after a storm means walking outside first and calling the gas utility or 911.
  • Keep children and pets out of rooms where water came from outside.

Once the scene is safe, photograph the water, the entry point and the damaged items. Then call us.

Storm water, flood water and roof leaks are different jobs

Water that blows in through a window or door during a storm is one thing. Water that rises from outside and covers the floor is another, and that work belongs with flood damage restoration. A slow drip from a damaged roof is handled on the roof leak page.

Rain that came from outside is not clean water. It carries soil, debris and whatever the runoff crossed. That matters for porous material. Carpet pad, insulation and the bottom of a drywall wall are usually removed, not dried in place.

How we handle storm water damage

We work from the outside in, and we do not guess. Industry practice under ANSI/IICRC S500 guides how we classify the water and set drying goals.

  1. Walk-through and photos. We find the entry point, note the water's source and record the starting condition.
  2. Extraction. Truck-mount and portable extractors pull standing water and soaked carpet before it spreads. This is the same step described on our emergency water removal page.
  3. Moisture mapping. Moisture meters and a thermal camera show wet drywall, framing and subfloor that look dry.
  4. Removal of what cannot dry. Contaminated pad, wet insulation and low drywall come out with flood cut tools. Cavities that can be saved get opened or ported.
  5. Drying. Equipment stays in place until the meter readings return to the dry reference. If the space is open or damp, desiccant units may be added.
  6. Cleaning and air control. We clean affected surfaces and use HEPA air scrubbers when dust or odor is an issue.
  7. Documentation. You get readings, photos and a record of what was removed for your adjuster.

Our side of a storm loss is extraction, drying, removal of wet materials and photo records. A roofer repairs or tarps the roof, and a plumber fixes any broken line.

How storm claims usually work

Policies differ, so ask your agent or adjuster how yours treats wind, hail and water. In general, a sudden storm opening is handled differently from rising flood water, and wear on an older roof is handled differently from a damaged one.

Photos taken before cleanup help most, along with notes on when the storm hit and when you first saw water. We add moisture readings and a removal log. We do not promise coverage or approval.

Mistakes to avoid after a storm

  • Waiting for the sky to clear before calling. Water keeps spreading while you wait.
  • Running a box fan over soaked carpet and calling it done.
  • Throwing out damaged items before taking photos.
  • Walking through standing water near live power.
