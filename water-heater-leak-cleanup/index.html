Where water heater leaks come from

Not every leak at a water heater means the tank has failed. The source decides how much water you are dealing with and how fast it arrives.

Tank failure

A tank can corrode through at the bottom or develop a seam leak. This usually releases the most water, potentially a full tank, and it does not stop on its own.

Fittings and connections

Supply lines, flex connectors and unions at the top can loosen or corrode. These tend to drip slowly and keep dripping for as long as nobody notices.

Relief valve and drain valve

The temperature and pressure relief valve can release water if pressure builds, and the drain valve near the base can weep. Both are plumber items, and a slow weep can hide for weeks under a closet floor.

Condensation

Some gas and high-efficiency units make condensate that must drain away. If that line clogs, water spills out and can look like a tank leak.

Where the heater sits changes the damage

If your heater sits in a garage, water spreads over the slab and wicks into the bottom of wall framing and anything stored on the floor. If it sits in a closet, water moves into carpet, closet walls and the rooms next to it.

If it sits in the attic, water drops through insulation and the ceiling below, so the first sign may be a stain in a hallway or bedroom far from the heater. We trace the path with a thermal camera and moisture meters instead of trusting the stain.

First-hour safety steps

A heater leak mixes water, electricity or gas, and very hot water.

  • Close the cold-water shutoff valve on the pipe feeding the heater. If it will not turn, close the main at the house. If the main is stuck, call the Town rather than forcing the meter.
  • Electric heater: switch off its breaker, but only if you can reach the panel without standing in water. Do not touch the heater or its wiring while wet.
  • Gas heater: turn the gas control to off. If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 from outside, and do not flip switches.
  • Do not stand in water near live power. If water has reached outlets, call an electrician or 911.
  • Water released near the relief valve or from the tank can be hot enough to scald.
  • Photograph the heater, its label and the wet areas before moving anything.

How we clean up and dry after a heater leak

Water from a tank starts as clean supply water, but rust, sediment and insulation change how we handle it. Our process follows industry practice under ANSI/IICRC S500.

  1. Map the wet area. Moisture meters and a thermal camera show where water went, including under cabinets, behind baseboards and above a ceiling.
  2. Extract. Truck-mount and portable extractors, with a weighted wand on carpet, remove standing water from floors.
  3. Remove what cannot dry. Wet carpet pad, soaked insulation and baseboards come out. Drywall that is soft or stained through gets a flood cut above the wet line.
  4. Dry. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers dry the room. Injectidry cavity drying ports move air into wall cavities and behind trim.
  5. Clean the surfaces. Rust and sediment are cleaned from floors and hard surfaces that stay, and the air is cleaned with HEPA air scrubbers where dust was disturbed.
  6. Document. You receive photos and moisture readings from first visit to dry finish for your insurer.

This sequence mirrors our water mitigation service, and the wider cleanup work is described on water damage cleanup. If a washer or dishwasher caused the leak instead, see appliance leak water damage.

A tank that fails suddenly is often viewed differently from a drip that ran for weeks. Policies differ, so ask your agent or adjuster whether the water damage, the heater and the plumber's repair are treated separately.

Mistakes to avoid and simple prevention

  • Leaving wet carpet pad in place because the top looks dry.
  • Relying on a wet-vac and a fan while the wall base stays damp.
  • Closing up a closet wall before the cavity reads dry.
  • Ignoring rust, a weeping valve or a damp closet floor.
  • Prevention: know where the shutoff valve is, ask a plumber about a drain pan and leak sensor, and have the heater checked as it ages.
